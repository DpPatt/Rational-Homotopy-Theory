\section{Homology and Cohomology}

In this section we briefly review Homology and Cohomology.
Think of it like a crash course on these topics.
For more details you can always refer to \cite{Hatcher2002}. 
As we had discussed before, the Homology and Cohomology are the functors from the category of topological space \(\topsp\) to category of graded abelian group \(\GrAb\) and category of graded ring \(\GrRing\) respectively.

There is two variants of chain complex, one is increasing chain complex and other is decreasing chain complex.
A decreasing chain complex \(C\) of abelian groups is a sequence(or tower) 
\[\dots \xrightarrow{\partial_{i+2}} C_{i+1} \xrightarrow{\partial_{i+1}} C_{i} \xrightarrow{\partial_{i}} C_{i-1} \xrightarrow{f_{i-1}} \dots,\]
where \(C_i\)'s are abelian groups(called chain groups) indexed by integers, and \(\partial_i\)'s are group homomorphisms(called the boundary maps) with \(\partial_{i}\partial_{i+1} = 0, \forall\ i \in \mathbb Z\), in other words,
\[\im(\partial_{i+1}) \subseteq\ker(\partial_{i}).\]
We refer these maps \(\partial\) as boundary maps.
The \(n\)-th homology of a the complex \(C\) is defined as the the quotient
\[H_n(C) = \ker(\partial_n)/\im(\partial_{n-1}).\]
An element of kernel is called a cycle, and that of image is called boundary. 

The increasing chain complex of abelian groups is the same setup as above but the maps points towards the group having higher index.
In increasing chain complex we sometime refer the maps as coboundary maps, and the abelian groups in the complex are called cochain groups.
An element of kernel is sometime called a cocycle, and that of image is called coboundary.
In case of increasing chain complex we sometime call the homology as cohomology.

\begin{definition}[Exact Sequence]
	A sequence of abelian groups \(E\) is called exact if it's homology group \(H_n(E)\) is trivial for all \(n\).
	
	A short exact sequence is an exact sequence of the form 
	\[0 \to A \to B \to C \to 0.\]
	Notice that the map \(A \to B\) is injective and \(B \to C\) is surjective.
\end{definition}
Examples are
\begin{equation}
	\label{nonsplit}
	0 \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \to \mathbb Z/4 \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \to 0,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	\label{split}
	0 \to A \to A \oplus B \to B \to 0.
\end{equation}
Notice that (\ref{nonsplit}) is a short exact sequence but \(\mathbb{Z}/4 \not\cong \mathbb Z/2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\).
The short exact sequence of the form (\ref{split}) is called a split short exact sequence.
The notion of split short exact sequence is completely different in case of non abelian group,	 where a short exact sequence can split in two different ways called left split and right split separately.
We are not going to talk about that in this notes.

\begin{definition}[Chain maps]
	A chain map \(f: A \to B\) between chain complexes \(A\) and \(B\) of same type (i.e., either both increasing or both decreasing) is a collection of maps
	\[f_n: A_n \to B_n \ \forall\ n,\]
	such that following diagram commutes for all \(n\),
	\[\begin{tikzcd} 
		A_n \ar{r}{\partial_n} \ar{d}[swap]{f_n} & A_{n+1} \ar{d}{f_{n+1}} \\ 
		B_n \ar{r}[swap]{\partial_n} & B_{n+1} 
	  \end{tikzcd}\]
\end{definition}
A chain map induces a map in homology 
\[f_\ast: H_\ast(A) \to H_\ast(B).\]

\subsection{Singular chain complex}
For a space \(X\) the Singular chain complex is a decreasing chain complex \(C(X)\) of abelian groups, where the chain groups \(C_n(X)\) is abelian group generated by the set of all continuous function \( \Delta^n \to X\) from \(n\)-simplex to \(X\).
So elements in \(C_n(X)\) is a formal \(\mathbb Z\)-linear finite sum of continuous functions \( \Delta^n \to X\), i.e., \(\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^k a_i\sigma_i, a_i \in \mathbb Z\) and \(\sigma_i:\Delta^n \to X\) is a continuous function.
The boundary map \[\partial_n:C_n(X) \to C_{n-1}(X),\] is a \(\mathbb Z\)-linear map defined on the generators by 
\[\partial_n(\sigma) = \sum_{i=0}^n (-1)^i (\sigma \circ d_i).\] 
\begin{exercise}
	Prove that \(\partial^2 = 0\).
\end{exercise}
Homology of this chain complex \(C(X)\) is called the singular homology \(H_\ast(X)\) of \(X\).
A first time reader might find it troublesome to be a alternating sum rather than just a sum without any sign to worry about.
You can just suppress such thoughts by saying that, okay this alternating sum definition of the boundary maps works out and it makes \(\partial^2 =0\), and the other definition (without any sign) don't make \(\partial^2 =0\).
But such answer does not give any intuition about the topics. 
So here is my intuitive(hand wavy) and more fascinating reason behind it.
You may have understood by now that there is no coincidence that we call the map \(\partial\) a boundary map, we call it boundary map cause it picks the boundary of the simplex \(\Delta^n\).
The boundary of \(\Delta^n\) is an \(n-1\) dimensional orientable manifold, it is even homotopic to \(\mathbb S^{n-1}\) with a triangulation.
Yes, the description of \(\partial\) if you define without any sign, it picks out the boundary, but it is not accurate with respects to the orientation, but the alternating sum is good with orientation.
To convince you observe the following:
The 
\begin{exercise}
	Prove that \(H_\ast(-):\topsp \to \GrAb\) defines a functor.
\end{exercise}

\subsection{Simplicial homology}
The singular chain groups are usually enormous.
There is a alternative approach to find homology of a space.
There are certain space \(X\) that can be given a structure of either a simplicial complex or a \(\Delta\) complex, obtained by gluing together simplices \(\sigma\) of various dimensions along faces.

\begin{definition}[Simplicial Complex]
	A simplicial complex \(\mathcal K\) is a set of simplices that satisfy
		\begin{enumerate}
			\item Every face of a simplex in \(\mathcal K\) is in \(\mathcal K\).
			\item The non-empty intersection of two simplices \(\sigma_1, \sigma_2\) in \(\mathcal K\) is a face of both \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\). 
		\end{enumerate}
	The simplicial complex is a topological space.
	A simplicial structure on a space \(X\) is a homeomorphism from a simplicial complex to the space \(X\).
	
	There is a more abstract version of simplicial complex, loosely speaking a simplicial complex without an abstract geometry. Here is the definition of it.
	
	A simplicial complex consists of a set \(\mathcal S\) of subsets of a set \(V\) ("vertices") such that 
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \(\mathcal S\) contains all singleton subset of \(V\).
		\item If \(S \subseteq \mathcal S\) and \(T \subseteq S\), then \(T \in \mathcal S\).
	\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}


\begin{definition}[\(\Delta\) complex]
	A \(\Delta\) complex structure on a space \(X\)	is a collection of maps \(\sigma_\alpha: \Delta^n \to X\) (called simplex maps) with \(n\) depending on the index \(\alpha\) such that 
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item the restriction of \(\sigma_\alpha\) to the interior in injective.
		\item each point of \(X\) is the image of exactly one of such restriction.
		\item \(\sigma \circ d_k : \Delta^{n-1} \to X\) is one of the map in the collection, for face maps \(d_k\).
		\item A set \(A \subseteq X\) is open iff \(\sigma_\alpha^{-1}(A)\) is open in \(\Delta^n\) for all \(\alpha\).
	\end{enumerate}  
\end{definition}

\begin{exercise}
	A simplicial complex structure on \(X\) is a \(\Delta\)-complex structure whose simplicial maps are determined by its boundary. 
	More precisely, if we have two maps \(\sigma_\alpha: \Delta^n \to X\) and \(\sigma_\beta: \Delta^n \to X\) in the collection with \(\sigma_\alpha \circ d_k = \sigma_\beta \circ d_k\) for all \(k = 0, 1, \dots, n\), then \(\sigma_\alpha = \sigma_\beta\).  
\end{exercise}

By this we understand that there is more freedom how simplices can be glued in \(\Delta\) complex compared to in a simplicial complex.

We define \(n\)-th simplicial chain group for any \(\Delta\)-complex \(X\) as the abelian group generated by the set of all simplex maps \( \sigma_\alpha: \Delta^n \to X\).
Elements in \(C^\Delta_n(X)\) is a formal \(\mathbb Z\)-linear finite sum of simplex maps, i.e., \(\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^k a_i\sigma_i, a_i \in \mathbb Z\) and \(\sigma_i:\Delta^n \to X\) is a \(n\)-simplex map on \(X\).
The boundary map \[\partial_n:C^\Delta_n(X) \to C^\Delta_{n-1}(X),\] is a \(\mathbb Z\)-linear map defined on the generators by 
\[\partial_n(\sigma_\alpha) = \sum_{i=0}^n (-1)^i (\sigma_\alpha \circ d_i).\]
It is easy to see that \(\partial^2=0\).
And the \(n\)-th homology group of this complex is called the \(\Delta\)-complex homology of \(X\), \(H^\Delta_\ast(X)\).
Notice that the way we defined this homology group, it seems that the \(\Delta\)-complex homology of \(X\) depends on two things, one is the space itself and other is its \(\Delta\) complex structure.
The theorem \(2.27\) of \cite{Hatcher2002} proves the following:
\begin{theorem}
	The \(\Delta\) complex homology of a space \(X\) is independent of the \(\Delta\) complex structure on \(X\), and is isomorphic to the singular homology of \(X\).
\end{theorem}

Since every simplicial complex is a \(\Delta\) complex, we can now compute the homology of a space if it has a simplicial structure on it.
We call this homology as simplicial homology, and it will be isomorphic to the \(\Delta\) complex homology.

\begin{example}
	\[H_i(\text{point}) = \begin{cases}
		\mathbb Z & i = 0\\
		0 & \text{else.}
	\end{cases}\]
	\[H_i(\sphere{1}) = \begin{cases}
		\mathbb Z & i = 0,1\\
		0 & \text{else.}
	\end{cases}\]
	\[H_i(\sphere{n}) = \begin{cases}
		\mathbb Z & i = 0,n\\
		0 & \text{else.}
	\end{cases}\]
\end{example}

\subsection{Reduced homology:}
For convenience it is very useful to have a alternate version of homology when our space \(X\) is non-empty.
Think of it like homology of based spaces.
Given a non empty set \(X\) consider the chain complex
\[\dots \to C_2(X) \xrightarrow{\partial_1} C_1(X) \xrightarrow{\partial_0} C_0(X) \xrightarrow{\delta} \mathbb{Z} \to 0,\]
where \(\delta\) is defined as 
\[\delta\left(\sum_{i=1}^k a_i\sigma_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^k a_i.\]
The homology of this chain complex is called the reduced homology of \(X\) and denoted as \(\tilde H_\ast(X)\).

Notice that \(H_n(X) = \tilde H_n(X)\) for \(n \geq 1\).
For \(n =0\), the rank of \(\tilde{H}_0(X)\) is one less than the rank of \(H_0(X)\).
So when \(X\) is path connected, \(\tilde{H}_0(X)\) is trivial.

\subsection{Relative homology}
For \(A \subseteq X\) be a subspace of \(X\), then we define the relative chain complex \(C_\ast(X,A)\), where the relative chain groups are quotient \(C_n(X,A) \coloneq C_n(X)/C_n(A)\).
The boundary map \(\partial_n: C_n(X) \to C_{n-1}(X)\) restricts to a boundary map \(\partial_n: C_n(A) \to C_{n-1}(A)\), and hence it induces a relative boundary map 
\[\partial_n: C_n(X,A) \to C_{n-1}(X,A),\]
with \(\partial^2 = 0\).
The homology of this chain complex is called the relative homology of the pair \((X,A)\) and denoted as \(H_\ast(X,A)\).
If \(A \subseteq X\) is "nice"\footnote{By "nice" here we mean the inclusion \(i: A \to X\) is a cofibration. An example of such an inclusion is, if \(A\) is a deformation retract of some neighborhood in \(X\).} then \(H_\ast(X,A)\) is isomorphic to the reduced homology of the quotient space, i.e. \(\tilde{H}_\ast(X/A)\).
This is due to the excision theorem, i.e., theorem 2.20 of \cite{Hatcher2002}.
This shows that if we fix \(x_0 \in X\), relative homology \(H_\ast(X, x_0) \cong \tilde{H}_\ast(X)\).
This is why we said to think of reduced homology as the homology of the based space.

The basic properties of homology are:

\begin{theorem}[Homotopy Invariance]
	If \(f,g: X \to Y\) are homotopic maps then they induce the same maps in the homology \(f_\ast = g_\ast\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}
	If \(X\) and \(Y\) are homotopy equivalent then \(H_\ast(X) \cong H_\ast(Y)\) are isomorphic.
\end{corollary}
Using this corollary we can show that \(\real{m}\) is not homeomorphic to \(\real{n}\) if \(n \neq m\).

\begin{theorem}[LES of Pairs]
	There is a long exact sequence(LES) of pairs \((X,A)\) given as 
	\[\dots \to H_{n+1}(X,A) \xrightarrow{\partial_\ast} H_n(A) \xrightarrow{i_\ast} H_n(X) \xrightarrow{j_\ast} H_n(X,A) \xrightarrow{\partial_\ast} H_{n-1}(A) \to \dots\]
	for more about the maps refer to \cite{Hatcher2002}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{exercise}
	Calculate the homology groups of \(\sphere{n}\) using the LES of Pair. 
\end{exercise}

\begin{theorem}[Excision Theorem]
	\label{Excision}
	If \(U \subseteq Y \subseteq X\) is such that the closure of \(U\) is contained in the interior of \(Y\), then we have 
	\[H_\ast(X\backslash U,Y \backslash U) \cong H_\ast(X,Y).\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Mayer–Vietoris sequence]
	\label{MV-Sequence}
	If a space \(X\) has a decomposition as \(X = U \cup V\) with \(U, V\) open in \(X\), then there is a long exact sequence 
	\[\dots \to H_{n+1}(X) \xrightarrow{\partial_\ast} H_n(U\cap V) \xrightarrow{i_\ast} H_n(U) \oplus H_n(V) \xrightarrow{j_\ast} H_n(X) \xrightarrow{\partial_\ast} H_{n-1}(U\cap V) \to \dots\]
\end{theorem}

The \cref{Excision} and \cref{MV-Sequence} are equivalent.
If we have two homology theories with one containing \cref{MV-Sequence} in its axioms and other containing \cref{Excision} in its axiom, then both the theories are same.



\section{Cohomology}
	If we apply the functor \(\Hom(-, \mathbb Z)\) to the chain complex for homology, we obtain a cochain complex 
	\[\dots \to \Hom(C_{n-1}(X), \mathbb Z) \xrightarrow{\partial_n^\ast} \Hom(C_n(X), \mathbb Z) \xrightarrow{\partial_{n+1}^\ast} \Hom(C_{n+1}(X), \mathbb Z) \to \dots,\]
	We denote the cochian groups as \(C^n(X) = C^n(X;\mathbb Z) \coloneq \Hom(C_n(X), \mathbb Z)\), and the coboundary maps as \(\delta_n = \partial_{n}^\ast\).
	So the above cochain complex becomes
	\[\dots \to C^{n-1}(X;\mathbb Z) \xrightarrow{\delta_n} C^n(X;\mathbb Z) \xrightarrow{\delta_{n+1}} C^{n+1}(X;\mathbb Z) \to \dots.\]
	For \(\alpha \in C^{n-1}(X;\mathbb Z)\),
	\[\delta_n(\alpha) = \alpha\circ \partial_n\]
 	Since \(\partial^2 = 0\), we get \(\delta^2 = 0\).
 	The homology of the above cochain complex is called the cohomology of the space \(X\).
 	The type of cohomology we get is depended upon the chain complex for the homology we choose.
 	If we follow the above process for singular chain complex then we get singular cohomology, we denote is as \(H^\ast(X;\mathbb Z)\).
 	If it is the \(\Delta\)-complex chain complex we choose, then we get \(\Delta\)-complex cohomology \(H^\ast_\Delta(X, \mathbb Z)\).
 	Similarly for simplicial cohomology, reduced cohomology.
 	All of these cosmologies are isomorphic.
 	
 	We also have notion of relative cohomology, long exact sequence of pairs and Mayer-Vietoris sequence for cohomology, with all arrows reversed. 
 	
 	We can define homology \(H_\ast(X; G)\) with coefficients in any abelian groups \(A\) as the homology of the chain complex 
 	\[\dots \to C_n(X) \otimes G \to C_{n-1}(X) \otimes G \to \dots.\]
 	We can also define cohomology with coefficients \(H^\ast(X; G)\) with coefficient in abelian group \(G\) as the cohomology of the cochain complex
 	\[\dots \to \Hom(C_n(X), G) \to \Hom(C_{n+1}(X), G) \to \dots.\]
 	If \(G\) is not a ring then we don't have a product structure in cohomology with coefficients \(H^\ast(X; G)\).
 	
 	There ae algebraic properties of cohomology:
 	\vspace{0.5 cm}
 	
 	\begin{enumerate}
 		\item Cohomology usually comes with a \emph{graded product structure} in it called cup product.
 		For singular cochains it is defined as follows:
 		\begin{align*}
 			C^k(X) \times C^\ell(X) &\xrightarrow{\smile} C^{k + \ell}(X)\\
 			(\phi,\psi) &\mapsto \phi \smile \psi,
 		\end{align*}
 		\[(\phi\smile \psi)(\sigma) = \phi(fr_k(\sigma)).\psi(bk_{\ell}(\sigma)),\]
 		where \(fr_k(\sigma)\) is the restriction of \(\sigma\) to the  \(k\)-dimensional face of \(\Delta^{k +\ell}\) spanned by the first \(k+1\) vertices \(\{v_0, \dots v_k\}\), and \(bk_\ell(\sigma)\) is the restriction of \(\sigma\) to the \(\ell\) dimensional face of \(\Delta^{k+\ell}\) spanned by last \(\ell + 1\) vertices \(\{v_k, \dots, v_{k+\ell}\}\) (with appropriate identification of simplex).
 		
 		This product is associative, bilinear, unital in the cochain level.
 		It descends to a maps
 		\[H^k(X, \mathbb Z) \otimes H^\ell(X, \mathbb Z) \to H^{k + \ell}(X, \mathbb Z).\]
 		This map is graded commutative on cohomology, but not in the cochain.
 		The cup product satisfy the Leibniz rule in homology(not in chain level):
 		for \(\alpha \in H^k(X)\) and \(\beta \in H^\ell (X)\)
 		\[\delta(\alpha \smile \beta) = \delta(\alpha) \smile \beta + (-1)^k \alpha \smile \delta(\beta).\]
 		\item There is a cap product:
 		\[H^k(X) \otimes H_\ell(X) \xrightarrow{\frown} H_{\ell-k}(X),\]
 		defined in the chain level 
 		\[\phi \frown \sigma = \phi(fr_k(\sigma)) bk_{\ell - k}(\sigma).\]
 		
 		\item For space \(X, Y\) and a commutative ring with unity \(R\) external cup product is a map 
 		\[H^\ast(X,R) \otimes H^\ast(Y,R) \xrightarrow{\times} H^\ast(X\times Y , R),\]
 		given by \(\phi \times \psi = \pi_1^\ast(\phi) \smile \pi_2^\ast(\psi)\), where \(\pi_1,\pi_2\) are projection maps.
 		
 		\item There is a co-associative, graded-commutative co-multiplication structure for homology with field coefficients
 		\[\Delta:  H_n(X; \mathbb F) \to \bigoplus_{i+j = n} H_i(X; \mathbb F) \otimes H_j(X; \mathbb F),\]
 		induced by diagonal map \(\Delta : X \to X \times X\) and it is dual to the cup product in cohomology.
 		This is not true with integer coefficients.
 	\end{enumerate}
 	
 	
 	\subsection{Universal Coefficient Theorems}
 	There are two versions of this theorem, one for homology and other for cohomology.
 	It is called Universal Coefficient Theorem because it express homology  and cohomology with arbitrary coefficients in terms of homology with \(\mathbb Z\) coefficients.
 	
 	The free resolution of an abelian group \(H\) is the exact sequence 
 	\[\dots \to F_2 \to F_1 \to F_0 \to H,\]
 	with each \(F_i\) free.
 	If we dualize this sequence by functor \(\Hom(-, \mathbb G)\) we get a chain complex( not an exact sequence) which is exact at \(0\)-th position. 
 	\[\dots \leftarrow F_2^\ast \leftarrow F_1^\ast \leftarrow F_0^\ast \leftarrow H^\ast.\]
 	We define \(\Ext(H,G)\) the first cohomology group of the above sequence (other homologies are trivial).
 	This groups is independent of the free resolution of the group \(H\).
 	It only depends upon the group \(H\) and \(G\).
 	This bifunctor \(\Ext(-,-)\) has some nice property and it is highly computable.
 	The Universal Coefficient Theorem for cohomology states the following:
 	\begin{theorem}
 		There is a surjective map \( H^n(X;G) \to \Hom(H_n(X),G)\) with kernel \(\Ext(H_{n-1}(X), G)\).
 		Hence, there is a short exact sequence 
 		\[0 \to \Ext(H_{n-1}(X), G) \to H^n(X;G) \to \Hom(H_n(X),G) \to 0.\]
 		And this short exact sequence splits but not naturally.
 		This sequence is natural with respects to the continuous maps \(X \to Y\).
 	\end{theorem}
 	
 	This gives us some helpful corollaries given below.
 	\begin{corollary}
 		If \(H_n(X)\) is finitely generated for for each \(n\), then 
 		\[H^n(X;\mathbb Z) \cong H_n(X;\mathbb Z)/T(H_n(X;\mathbb Z)) \bigoplus T(H_{n-1}(X;\mathbb Z)),\]
 		Where \(T(H)\) is the torsion subgroup of group \(H\).
 	\end{corollary}
 	\begin{corollary}
 		For any field \(\mathbb F\) of characteristic zeros, 
 		\[H^n(X;\mathbb F) \cong H_n(X;\mathbb F).\] 
 	\end{corollary}
 	
 	If we apply the functor \(- \otimes G\) to the free resolution of the group \(H\) we get an chain complex that is exact at \(0\)-th position,
 	\[\dots \to F_2 \otimes G \to F_1 \otimes G \to F_0 \otimes G \to H \otimes G.\]
 	We define \(\Tor(H,G)\) the first homology group of the sequence (other homologies are trivial).
 	This groups is independent of the free resolution of the group \(H\).
 	It only depends upon the group \(H\) and \(G\).
 	This bifunctor \(\Tor(-,-)\) has some nice property and it is highly computable.
 	Now the Universal Coefficient Theorem for homology.
 	\begin{theorem}
 		There is a (non natural) split short exact sequence 
 		\[0 \to H_n(X) \otimes G \to H_n(X;G) \to \Tor(H_{n-1}(X), G) \to G.\]
 		This sequence is natural with respects to the continuous maps \(X \to Y\).
 	\end{theorem}
 	
 	\subsection{K\"unneth Formula}
 	The K\"unneth formulas describe the homology or cohomology of a product space in terms of the homology or cohomology of the factors.
 	There is more general versions of K\"unneth formula for homology and cohomology involving \(\Tor\) and \(\Ext\) terms.
 	Here, we are going to talk about a special case of those versions.
 	
 	\begin{theorem}
 	For the CW- complexes \(X, Y\) the cross product is an isomorphism of rings if the cohomology group \(H^k(Y, R)\) is finitely generated free \(R\)-module for all \(k\). 	
 	\end{theorem}
 	Equivalently, we can define cross product map for homologies
 	\[H_\ast(X,R) \otimes H_\ast(Y,R) \xrightarrow{\times} H_\ast(X\times Y , R).\]
 	This map is an isomorphism if \(H_i(X,R)\) is free for all \(i\).
 	
 	\subsection{Poincar\'e Duality}
 	This relates the homology and cohomology of a compact, orientable manifold.
 	This says
 	\begin{theorem}
 		For compact, oriented, \(n\)-dimensional manifold there is a isomorphism 
 		\[PD: H^k(M) \to H_{n-k}(M),\]
 		which is given by the cap product with the fundamental class \([M] \in H_n(M)\).
 	\end{theorem}
 	
 	For a manifold \(X\) the cup product \(a \smile b\) can sometimes be calculated in terms of signed intersection of cycles.
 	For example, on the space \(X = S^1 \times S^1\)
 	
 	\begin{align*}
 		&H^2(X) \stackrel{PD}{\cong} H_0(X) \cong \mathbb Z, &\text{ generates by }\ 1,\\
 		&H^1(X) \stackrel{PD}{\cong} H_1(X) \cong \mathbb Z + \mathbb Z, &\text{ lets say generated by }\ a, b,\\
 		&H^2(X) \stackrel{PD}{\cong} H_0(X) \cong \mathbb Z, &\text{ lets say generated by }\ c.
 	\end{align*}
 	Then, \(c\smile c = a \smile c = b \smile c = 0\) because higher cohomology groups are trivial and 
 	\(a \smile b = c\).
 	\vspace{0.5 cm}

 	If \(M\) is a smooth manifold then \(H^\ast(M;\mathbb R)\) can be computed as cohomology of the de Rham chain complex \(C_{dR}(M)\) of differential forms.
 	In this complex, the product is graded commutative in cochain level.
 	For more details look at \cite{Bott_Tu1982}
