\section{CW-Complexes}
For a space \(Y\) and any map \(f: \partial	\mathbb D^n \to Y\) we can form a space 
\[Y \cup_f \mathbb D^n \coloneq (Y \sqcup \mathbb D^n)/\sim\]
where \(x \sim f(f(x))\) for all \(x \in \partial \mathbb D^n\).
This is the resulting space of attaching a \(n\)-cell to \(Y\) via attaching map \(f\).

\begin{definition}[Cell complex]
	By cell complex \(\mathcal K\) we mean a sequence of space
	\[\varnothing = K^{(-1)} \subseteq K^{(0)} \subseteq K_{(1)} \subseteq \dots \subseteq K^{(n)} \subseteq \dots,\]
	where \(K^{(0)}\) is the discrete set of points, and \(K^{(n)}\) (called the \(n\)-th skeleton) is obtained form \(K^{(n-1)}\) by attaching \(n\)-cells to \(K^{(n-1)}\) via some attaching maps, and we also have 
	\[\bigcup_{n \geq 0} K^{(n)} = K.\]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	A CW-complex \(X\) is a cell complex satisfying the following two properties:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \emph{Closure Finiteness:}  The closure of each \(n\)-cell in \(X\) intersects with only finitely many other simplex.
		\item \emph{Weak Topology:}  A subset \(U \subseteq X\) is open if \( A \cap\,  e_\alpha\) is open for all cells \(e_\alpha\) in the complex \(X\).
	\end{enumerate}

	The CW stands for the "\textbf{C}losure finite \textbf{W}eak topology."
	The CW-complex satisfy the closure finite property, that says the closure of every cell in the complex intersects only a finite number of other cells.
	This property ensures that the space remains locally well-behaved and compact subsets only interact with finitely many cells, preventing wild, infinite fragmentation (such as a single cell whose boundary closure wraps around infinitely many discrete points).
	For more details refer to \cite[p.~221-223]{Whitehead_1949}.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
	For all \(n \geq 1\), \(\sphere{n}\) has a CW-complex structure with one \(0\)-cell and one \(n\)-cell.
	It can also be given a CW structure with two \(k\)-cells for all \(k = 0, 1, \dots, n\).
	
	The real projective space \(\rproj{n}\) has a CW structure with one \(k\)-cell for all \(k \in \{0, 1, \dots, n\}\).
	
	The complex projective space \(\cproj{n}\) has a CW structure with one \(k\)-cell for all even \(k \in \{0, 2, \dots, 2n\}\).
\end{example}

\begin{definition}[Homotopy Extension Property(HEP)]
	Let $i : A \to X$ be a continuous map.
	We say $i$ satisfy the homotopy extension property if for any homotopy $f: A \to Y^I$ and a map $\tilde{f}_0: X \to Y$ satisfying 
	\[\tilde{f}_0 \circ i = \mathrm{ev}_0 \circ f\]
	there exists an extension of $f$ to a homotopy
	\[\tilde{f} : X \to Y^I\] 
	such that 
	\[\tilde{f} \circ i = f.\]
	This can be expressed as the commutative diagram 
	\[\begin{tikzcd}
		A\ar[r, "f"]\ar[d, "i"'] & Y^I \ar[d, "\mathrm{ev}_0"] \\
		X \ar[r, "\tilde{f}_0"']\ar[ru, "\exists \tilde{f}", dashed]& Y
	\end{tikzcd}\]
	It is easy to see that such a map \(i: A \to X\) has to be an inclusion (exercise).
	So instead of saying \(i : A \to X\) satisfy HEP we can say that the pair \((X,A)\) satisfy the HEP with \(A \subseteq X\).
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
	For CW complex \(X\) and its sub-complex \(A\) the pair \((X,A)\) satisfy the HEP. 
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Simplicial Approximation]
	\label{simplical approx.}
	For all CW-complex \(X\) there is a simplicial complex \(K\) that is homotopy equivalent to \(X\).
\end{proposition}
For the proof see \cite[\S 2]{griffiths_rational_2013}. 
Proof uses the fact that CW pairs have HEP.

\begin{theorem}[CW-approximation theorem, \cite{Hatcher2002}]
	Let $X$ be a topological space $X$ there is a $CW$-complex $X_{CW}$ and a weak homotopy equivalence $f: X_{CW} \to X$. 
	This $CW$-complex is unique up to homotopy.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[sketch of the proof for existance]
	The proof is by induction.
	We can always find a set $A_0$ that is bijective to the path components of $X$ and a map $f: A_0 \to X$ such that given two $a,b \in A_0$, $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ don't lie in the same path component of $X$.
	Suppose we have defined the map $f_{k-1}: A_{k-1} \to X$, where $A_{k-1}$ is a $CW$-complex of dimension $\leq k-1$.
	In the $k$-th step if necessary we will glue $k$-cells (i.e. $D^k$) to $A_{k-1}$ in such a way that the map $f_{k-1}: A_{k-1} \to X$ can be extended to a map $f_k: A_k \to X$ (i.e. $f_k\mid A_{k-1} = f_{k-1}$) that satisfy the following: 
	For each base point $a \in A_0$,
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $(f_k)_\ast : \pi_{k-1}(A_k, a) \to \pi_{k-1}(X, f(a))$ is injective, and
		\item $(f_k)_\ast : \pi_{k}(A_k, a) \to \pi_{k}(X, f(a))$ is surjective.
	\end{enumerate}
	For the full proof see \cite[\S4.1]{Hatcher2002}.
\end{proof}
Let's do an example for a better understanding.
\begin{example}
	Let us consider $X = S^2$.
	\[\pi_n(S^2) = \left\{\begin{array}{ll}
		\mathbb{Z}, & n=2\\
		0, & n \neq 2. 
	\end{array}\right.\]
	In our proof $A_0$ is a singleton set $\{\ast\}$, and choose $f_0: \{\ast\} \to S^2$ as 
	\[f_0(\ast) = (0,0,1) \in S^2 \subseteq R^3.\]
	Notice that $(f_0)_\ast : \pi_1(\{\ast\}, \ast) \to \pi_1(S^2, f(\ast))$ is an isomorphism.
	Hence, for $k =1$, we don't need to glue any $1$-disks to $A_0$.
	We take $A_1 = A_0$ and $f_1 = f_0$.
	Notice that $f_1$ satisfy the property (1) and (2) given in the above proof.
	
	For $k =2$, we take a $2$-cell $D^2$, glue it to $A_1= \{\ast\}$ by the map $\partial D^2 \cong S^1 \to \{\ast\}$ to $A_1$.
	Then, $A_2 \cong S^2$ and extend the map $f_1: A_1 \to S^2$ to $f_2: A_2 \to S^2$ such that 
	\[f_2(\ast) = (0,0,1), \text{ it is a weak homotopy equivalence.}\]
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
	This theorem does not says that every space is homeomorphic to a CW complex.
	This only states the existence of a CW complex that is homotopy equivalent to the space.
	There are many spaces who do not have CW-structure on it, for example topologist's sine curve, Hawaiian ring and infinite dimensional Banach space etc.
\end{remark}

\subsection{CW Homology}
For a CW complex \(X\) there is a chain complex \(C_\ast^{CW}(X)\) where 
\[C_n^{CW}(X)\coloneq \mathbb Z\{n-\text{cells of }X\},\]
and the boundary map 
\[\partial_n: C_n^{CW}(X) \to C_{n-1}^{CW}(X)\]
is given by the composition
\[H_n(X^{(n)}, X^{(n-1)}) \xrightarrow{\partial} H_{n-1}(X^{(n-1)})  \xrightarrow{j_{n-1}} H_n(X^{(n-1)}, X^{(n-2)}).\]
This is induced by the attachiing maps of \(n\)-cells.
For a \(n\)-cell \(e_\alpha\), the collection of integers \(d_{\alpha\beta}\), where \(\beta \) runs over number of \((n-1)\) cells, that determines the boundary map \(\partial_n\) is given by the number of times \(\partial e_\alpha\) winds arround the \((n-1)\) cell \(e_\beta\)(counted with a sign) via the attaching map.
Homology of this chian complex is called cellular homology \(H_\ast^{CW}(X)\)of the complex \(X\).
\begin{theorem}
	The cellular homology \(H_\ast^{CW}(X)\) is isomorphic to the singular homology \(H_\ast(X)\).
\end{theorem}
For more details refer to \cite[\S 2.2]{Hatcher2002}.

\begin{example}
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item For \(X = \sphere{1} \times \sphere{1}\), the cellualr chain complex
		\(C_\ast^{CW}(X)\) is 
		\[0 \to \mathbb Z \xrightarrow{0} \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb Z \xrightarrow{0} \mathbb Z \to 0.\]
		\item For \(X = \rproj{n}\), the cellualr chain complex \(C_\ast^{CW}(X)\) is
		\[0 \to \mathbb Z \to \dots \to \mathbb Z \xrightarrow{2} \mathbb Z \xrightarrow{0} \mathbb Z\xrightarrow{2} \mathbb Z \xrightarrow{0} \mathbb Z \to 0.\]
		\item For \(X = \cproj{n}\), the cellualr chain complex \(C_\ast^{CW}(X)\) is
		\[0 \to \mathbb Z \to \dots \to \mathbb Z \xrightarrow{0} 0 \xrightarrow{0} \mathbb Z \xrightarrow{0} 0 \xrightarrow{0} \mathbb Z \to 0.\]
	\end{enumerate}
\end{example}

Let us end this section with few remarks.
So far we have learned spaces having certain special strucure on them.
We have learned simplicial complex, \(\Delta\)-complex, and CW-complex.
There are spaces that can be obtained from the "geometric realization" of "simplicial sets".
Simplical sets are kind of graded sets having some sort of degeneracy maps and face maps between consecutive levels.
We are not going to need them here, so we are not discussing them.

The topological manifolds have CW-complex structure on them, but you don't always find a simplicial structure (aka. triangulation) on a topological manifold.
The smooth manifolds a subclass of topological manifolds have simplical structure on them.
We have the following figure:
\[\begin{tikzcd}
	\{\text{Simplical Complex}\} \arrow[d, hook] &  & \{C^{\infty}\text{-Manifolds}\} \arrow[ll, hook'] \arrow[ddd, hook] \\
	\{\Delta\text{-Complex}\} \arrow[d, hook]    &  & \\
	\{\text{GR of SS}\} \arrow[d, hook]          &  & \\
	\{\text{CW-Complex}\}                        &  & \{\topsp\text{-Manifolds}\} \arrow[ll, hook']                      
\end{tikzcd}\]
Where by "GR of SS" we mean the Geometric realization of Simplical sets.
Each of the maps in above figure is an inclusion, and non of these inclusions are an equality.
Roughly speaking, for all of the above inclusions, the homotopy type of a space from the target is hit by some space from the source.
But sometimes one care about more than just homotopy type.
